Year-end closedown: leave, public holidays and pay over December

December is when small businesses make the most avoidable HR mistakes: leave forced on people who don't have it, public holidays paid wrongly, and contracts that quietly roll over. Here is what to sort out before the office closes.

1. Tell staff the closedown dates in writing, now

If you close between Christmas and New Year, staff need to know the dates early enough to plan. Put the dates in writing and say how the days will be treated: annual leave, paid closedown days on the business, or a mix.

2. You can decide when leave is taken, within limits

The Basic Conditions of Employment Act gives full-time employees at least 21 consecutive days of annual leave per leave cycle, which is 15 working days for a five-day week. Where there's no agreement on timing, the employer may decide when leave is taken. A closedown clause in your contracts or leave policy makes this far easier to apply fairly, so check yours says so.

For staff who don't have enough leave to cover the shutdown, you can agree to advance leave or agree on unpaid days. The key word is agree. Don't simply deduct the days from pay: deductions generally need the employee's written consent.

3. Know the public holidays in the period

DateHolidayFalls on
16 Dec 2026Day of ReconciliationWednesday
25 Dec 2026Christmas DayFriday
26 Dec 2026Day of GoodwillSaturday
1 Jan 2027New Year's DayFriday

Only a public holiday that falls on a Sunday moves to the Monday. The Day of Goodwill falls on a Saturday this year, so it doesn't move: staff who normally work Saturdays get it as a paid holiday, and Monday-to-Friday staff don't get an extra day off.

If a public holiday falls during someone's annual leave on a day they would normally have worked, it doesn't count as a leave day.

4. Working on a public holiday is by agreement, and costs more

Staff can only be required to work on a public holiday by agreement. If the holiday falls on a day they would ordinarily work, pay is at least double their normal daily wage. If you trade over the festive season, agree the roster in writing now rather than on the 15th.

5. Bonuses and 13th cheques

The law doesn't require a year-end bonus. It becomes an obligation when it's in the contract, or when it has been paid consistently enough to become an established practice. If you're changing what you pay this year, explain it early and apply it consistently across the team.

6. Fixed-term contracts that end in December

A contract that has been renewed before, or where the person was told they'd probably be kept on, can create a reasonable expectation of renewal. Not renewing it can then be treated as a dismissal. Decide now, and communicate the decision properly.

Your year-end checklist

  • Closedown dates confirmed and sent to staff in writing
  • Leave balances checked; shortfalls agreed with each person
  • Festive season roster agreed, with public holiday pay calculated
  • Bonus position decided and communicated
  • Fixed-term contracts ending in December reviewed
  • Emergency contact for HR issues over the break agreed

AI at work: what POPIA means for your business

Most of the worry about AI at work is whether it will take people's jobs. The more immediate risk for a small business is simpler: what your staff are putting into AI tools, and what AI devices are recording. The law hasn't changed. The tools have.

Your team is probably already using AI

If someone pastes a client email, a CV or a disciplinary note into a chatbot to "tidy it up", your business has just processed personal information through a third party, often on servers outside South Africa. The Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) applies to that exactly as it applies to your filing cabinet.

POPIA requires personal information to be processed lawfully, for a specific purpose, and kept secure. It also places conditions on sending personal information outside the country. A free AI tool with unclear data terms can make all three hard to show.

Wearable AI changes what "being recorded" means

Smart glasses and AI recording pins can capture faces, voices and conversations without anyone noticing. A person's image and voice are personal information, and biometric information is treated as special personal information under POPIA, with stricter rules. If staff wear these devices in meetings with clients or colleagues, you need a clear rule on when that's allowed and how people are told.

AI in recruitment needs a human in the loop

Tools that score or filter CVs automatically are attractive when you're hiring on a small budget. POPIA restricts decisions about a person that are based solely on automated processing where they have legal consequences or substantially affect that person. Use AI to help sort, but make sure a person reviews and makes the decision, and keep a record of why.

What the risk looks like

The Information Regulator can impose administrative fines of up to R10 million, and some POPIA offences carry prison sentences. For most small businesses the bigger cost is reputational: a client finding out their details were fed into a public AI tool.

Five things to put in place

  • An AI use policy: which tools are approved, and what information must never go into them
  • A rule on recording devices, including smart glasses, in meetings
  • Updated privacy notices for staff and job applicants that mention AI tools
  • A person reviewing every AI-assisted hiring or disciplinary decision
  • Your Information Officer registered with the Information Regulator, and staff knowing who it is

Your first disciplinary hearing: what the CCMA will look for

When a dismissal is challenged at the CCMA, the commissioner asks two questions. Was there a fair reason? And was the process fair? Owners often get the first right and lose on the second.

Before the hearing: is there a fair reason?

For misconduct, the questions are practical. Was there a rule? Did the employee know about it, or should they reasonably have known? Did they break it? Have you applied the same rule the same way to others? And is dismissal an appropriate response, or would a warning do? For a first, minor offence, the answer is usually a warning.

Poor performance is not misconduct. It needs a different process: clear standards, feedback, support and time to improve before dismissal is on the table.

The process: the steps that matter

  1. Investigate first. Collect the facts and statements before deciding there's a case.
  2. Give written notice of the allegations in a language the employee understands, with the date and time of the hearing.
  3. Allow reasonable time to prepare. Not the same afternoon.
  4. Allow representation by a fellow employee or, where there's a union, a shop steward.
  5. Use an impartial chairperson. The person who made the complaint shouldn't decide the outcome.
  6. Let the employee state their case, call witnesses and question yours.
  7. Give the decision and reasons in writing, and tell the employee they may refer a dispute to the CCMA or bargaining council within 30 days of dismissal.

The mistakes we see most

  • The owner chairs a hearing about their own complaint
  • No minutes or record of what was said
  • Dismissing for something others have been warned for
  • Treating poor performance as misconduct
  • Deciding the outcome before the hearing starts

The Code of Good Practice: Dismissal allows a less formal process for small businesses. It does not remove the need for a fair reason and a genuine chance for the employee to respond.

Do you need an HR manager yet? A guide by headcount

Most owners don't decide to get HR help. A problem decides for them: a CCMA referral, a key resignation, a team that stopped pulling together. Here is what usually changes as a business grows.

Up to about 10 people

The owner handles HR, and that's usually fine if the basics are in place. Every employee needs written particulars of employment, and you need to be registered for UIF and COIDA. Get a sound contract, a leave policy and a disciplinary code in place now; fixing them later is harder.

10 to 25 people

This is where DIY HR starts to strain. You now have managers who aren't the owner, roles that overlap, and performance issues that need a process. Job descriptions, probation reviews and a simple performance framework start paying for themselves. A few hours a week of experienced HR support is often enough.

25 to 50 people

Structure becomes the issue: who reports to whom, how pay decisions are made, and how you keep the culture as you hire. Employee relations matters take more time. Most businesses at this size benefit from a regular HR day or two each week.

50 to 100 people

Employment equity obligations apply to employers with 50 or more employees, including an employment equity plan and annual reports. HR becomes a standing function rather than an occasional task: policies, compliance reporting, workforce planning and steady recruitment. Many businesses at this size choose outsourced HR around three days a week, backed by specialists for labour law and IR, rather than building a department.

100 to 250 people

At this size HR needs a dedicated lead: someone on site most days who runs performance, employee relations and training, and reports to management every month. You can hire that person and build a team around them, or outsource the whole function: a dedicated HR lead four to five days a week, with labour law, IR and recruitment specialists behind them, without the cost of a full department.

250 people and more

Most businesses this size have an in-house HR team. The gaps tend to be project work and specialist depth: restructuring, union negotiations, executive search, or cover during a vacancy. That's where outside HR support adds the most.

The cost comparison

A full-time HR manager typically costs R25,000 to R45,000 or more a month in salary alone. A fractional HR executive gives you senior experience for the days you actually need, on a fixed monthly fee, and scales up or down as you grow.
